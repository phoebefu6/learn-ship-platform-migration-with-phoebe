Part 1 · covers what each check compares

Five checks between two warehouses

6 min live

Every check reads both warehouses and compares one thing. A check that reads one side only is a data-quality check, which the Testing Data and ML Code course owns; parity is always two-sided.

LiveExactly what the bench computes3 min▶
CheckComparesAlarm when
countsTotal rows in each warehouseThey differ
keysOrder keys on each side, and customer keys that do not resolveAny key missing or extra, a different number of duplicated keys, or a different number of unresolved customer keys
dailyOrders, item value, freight, items per order, reviewed orders and average review score, per dayAny value on any day is not exactly equal
parityThe same six metrics per monthAny month is outside Serai's signed tolerance for that metric
edgeThe day each side assigns to orders placed within an hour of local midnightAny of them lands on a different day

Serai's signed bar, a constructed policy: orders exact, item value within 0.05%, freight within 0.05%, items per order within 0.005, reviewed orders exact, average review score within 0.01. Rungs: 0 counts; 1 adds keys; 2 adds daily; 3 swaps daily for the signed bar at the monthly grain the board reads; 4 adds the edge check.

LiveHow the bench was checked2 min▶

A bench that runs in the page cannot hide a step in a notebook. The same engine file runs under node, and an independent pure-Python reference with the same definitions agrees with it on all 1,740 numbers of the ladder, signed bar, loose bar and break mode, to a relative tolerance of one part in a billion. The comparison itself was tested: a planted 0.1% fault on one number is caught. On load, the bench checks eight properties of its own, for example that the legacy totals reproduce the builder's reference to the cent and that the daylight-saving drift moves exactly the 72 orders a direct time-zone conversion moves, then plants a fault (a loader that drops one row) and confirms that three properties fail. A self-test that cannot fail measures nothing.

Real world

Tools that do some of this for you. Google's open-source Data Validation Tool lists "Column validation (count, sum, avg, min, max, stddev, group by)", row validation with SHA256 hashes, schema validation and custom-query validation. reladiff, "a fork of an archived project called data-diff", finds changed rows with "a divide-and-conquer algorithm, based on matching hashes". Both are moving projects: re-verify their current state before you depend on them. Neither decides your tolerances, your grain or your edge checks; those are the parts of the bench that are a judgement.

Build-along · the bench

Climb five rungs, then break it twice

★ 26 min · everyone builds

Click the rungs in order and say out loud what you expect before the table changes. Nothing here is random: the rows, the drifts and the checks are fixed, so your numbers will match the ones in the steps.

Rung 0, row counts. 1 of 5 defects caught. Only the late rows change the count: rows 8,614 vs 8,981. Every other drift leaves the count exactly equal.

Rung 1, add keys. 2 of 5. The late rows now also show as 367 duplicated keys, and the case-sensitive join appears: 1,767 customer keys unresolved, with not one row added or lost.

Rung 2, add every metric every day, exactly. 5 of 5, and 2 false alarms. Read the "what fired" column for the accepted differences: freight differs on 80 days from the per-line split, and floating-point money differs on 302 days of item value and 269 of freight, in the last bits.

Rung 3, replace exact days with the signed bar. 4 of 5, 0 false alarms. The fixed-offset drift is now missed: it moves 72 orders to the day before, and none of them crosses a month end, so every month is inside the bar.

Rung 4, add the edge check. 5 of 5, 0 false alarms: 72 of 595 near-midnight orders on another day. This is the suite to sign. Look at the "every drift at once" table too: orders 8,981, items per order 1.0000, average review score 4.0217 against 4.1390.

Press "Loosen the bar". At rung 4 the score falls to 4 of 5: the missing-review drift goes quiet, because its largest monthly gap is 2.22%, under 3%. At rung 3 it is 3 of 5. Then restore the signed bar and press the break button: 0 of 5 at every rung, because both sides now read the new warehouse.

★ Read the bench from the consoleSPM_LIVE.setRung(4); SPM_LIVE.setLoose(false); SPM_LIVE.setBreak(false); SPM_LIVE.current().score // { caught: 5, missed: 0, falseAlarms: 0, quiet: 3, ... } SPM_LIVE.setLoose(true); SPM_LIVE.current().score.caught // 4 SPM_LIVE.setLoose(false); SPM_LIVE.setBreak(true); SPM_LIVE.current().score.caught // 0
Part 2 · covers the anti-lever: a bar too loose

A tolerance wide enough to stop every alarm

5 min live

The loose bar never improves the score. That is its job: a bench must be able to print a worse number, or it measures nothing. It also shows why a tolerance is set per metric, against the size of the drift you need to see.

LiveSetting a tolerance per metric3 min▶

A count of reviewed orders has no rounding in it, so any difference is a defect: exact. Money summed in a different engine can differ in the last bits, so it needs a small relative tolerance, and Serai's 0.05% is far wider than the floating-point gaps (about 2e-10 BRL in a month) and far narrower than the late rows (BRL 1,197,842.24 against BRL 1,262,425.64 in total). An average needs an absolute tolerance on its own scale: Serai's 0.01 on a 1 to 5 score is narrow enough to see the missing-review drift, which moved one month by 0.0931.

Write each tolerance next to the drift it is meant to absorb and the drift it must still catch. A tolerance with no such sentence is a guess, and a guess is how 3% on every metric gets signed.

Real world

Row-by-row tools still need a bar. AWS's DMS validation "compares each row in the source with its corresponding row at the target" and "reports any mismatches"; Google's Data Validation Tool compares aggregates or row hashes. A tool reports differences. Which differences block cutover is a decision a person signs, before the first comparison runs, so that the result cannot move the bar.

Part 3 · covers the break button and the signed runbook

What each side read, and the signature

5 min live

The break button models a mistake that is easy to make and hard to see: a copied connection string points both sides of the comparison at the new warehouse. Every check passes, and the report is worthless.

Self-studyWhat the bench cannot tell you2 min▶

Each drift is planted alone, so the bench never shows two drifts cancelling each other in an aggregate, which happens in real moves. The six metrics are Serai's; yours will include ratios and distinct counts that behave differently under the same drifts. And the edge check exists because someone knew to look for time zones: it catches the drift you thought of. The checks that find the drift nobody thought of are the exact daily comparison at rung 2, read by a person, and the parallel run of Builder session 7.

Real world

Integrity first, written down in advance. GitHub's analysis of its October 2018 incident records the choice to "prioritize data integrity over site usability and time to recovery". Serai's runbook makes the same choice before cutover day, in writing: if the suite is not at rung 4 with the signed bar and both targets printed, the move waits.

Homework

The capstone

60 min
  1. Fill Serai's eight runbook sections with the decisions from sessions 1 to 9, each in one or two sentences, and sign section 4 with the bench's rung-4 numbers.
  2. Write your own parity bar: one line per metric your business reads, with its grain, its tolerance, the drift it absorbs and the drift it must still catch.
  3. Name one drift your own move could suffer that none of the seven covers, and design a check for it in the shape of the edge check: which rows, compared how, alarm when.
  4. Add both connection targets to the header of your parity report template, and ask a colleague to break it the way the break button does.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Builder session 10 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Rung 2 catches 5 of 5 defects; rung 3 catches 4. Why is rung 3 still the better base for the suite?

False alarms train people to ignore the suite. Rung 3 trades them for one known blind spot, the fixed-offset drift inside a month, and rung 4 is designed to see exactly that.

2 · With the loose bar on, rung 4 catches 4 of 5. Which defect slips through, and why?

Counts and keys are exact whatever the bar. Integer division moves items per order by far more than 3%. The 68 zeros move each month by 2.22% at most, so a 3% bar on every metric hides them.

3 · A parity report shows every check green. What must it also show before you trust it?

With both sides on the new warehouse every check is green and 0 of 5 defects are caught; with a loose bar a real defect is green too. The report is evidence only with its targets, rung and bar printed.
