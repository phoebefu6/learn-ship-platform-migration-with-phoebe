The week after: drift that hides

Readers moved, the rollback trigger stayed quiet, and the monthly numbers match. Part 1 · covers time-zone edges after cutover

A fixed offset moves the day, not the month

5 min live

The fixed-offset drift touches a tiny corner of the data: orders placed in the first hour of the local day, during the two Brazilian summers in the sample. Everywhere else the two conversions agree, which is why it survives every check that reads in bulk.

Live72 orders, 74 days, 0 months3 min▶

The drift as the bench plants it: a report layer turns the stored UTC instant into a local day by subtracting a fixed three hours. In Brazilian summer time the true offset was two hours, so an order placed at 00:30 local lands at 23:30 the day before. On Serai's sample that happens to 72 orders, all placed between 00:00 and 00:59 civil time inside the two windows, 2016-10-16 to 2017-02-18 and 2017-10-15 to 2018-02-17.

CheckWhat the fixed offset does to it
Row countsNothing: 8,614 rows both sides
KeysNothing: the same keys, none missing or extra
Daily, exactOrders differ on 74 of 598 days; item value on 81
Monthly, signed bar0 of 21 months fail: no shifted order crosses a month end
Whole-sample totalsUnchanged

Why 81 days for item value and only 74 for orders? Each moved order takes one from the day it left and adds one to the day before. When one order leaves a day and another arrives from the next, the count for that day is unchanged but its money is not, because the two orders are worth different amounts. You will see that cancellation happen to one day in tonight's build-along.

LiveWhy it can appear after cutover2 min▶

The parity suite proved the tables and reports it compared, and Serai's rung-4 suite would have caught this drift on any of them. The week after is different because conversions keep being written: a team rebuilds a year-on-year chart on the new warehouse, a report nobody listed gets ported in a hurry, an analyst writes a fresh query against the reloaded history. Each one picks its own way to turn an instant into a day, and none of them was in the suite. If one of them hard-codes -03:00, its monthly numbers match legacy and its daily numbers do not, and the first person to notice is whoever reads it by day.

New orders do not help you find it. Every order placed since Brazil stopped observing summer time converts the same way under both rules, so a week of clean new data says nothing about the reloaded history underneath it.

Self-studyThe month end that saved Serai, by luck2 min▶

"0 months" is a fact about this sample, not about the drift. An order placed at 00:20 local on the first day of a month in summer time moves to the last day of the month before, and then a monthly number moves too. Serai's 72 shifted orders simply contain no such case. Do not carry "a fixed offset never moves a month" into your own estate; homework 2 plants the case and lets you watch the monthly line change.

Real world

The rule changed, and the history kept the old one. The IANA time zone database's release 2019b records that "Brazil no longer observes DST." Any pipeline written since can hard-code -03:00 and pass every test on recent data. A migration is precisely the moment old data gets reloaded through new code, so it is precisely the moment that hard-coding meets the summers of 2016-17 and 2017-18. The same is true of any country whose rules have changed: the tz database keeps the history so that your SQL does not have to.

Part 2 · covers the time-zone edge check

Check the hour either side of midnight

5 min live

The edge check is narrow on purpose. It takes only the orders placed within an hour of local midnight, asks both warehouses which day each one belongs to, and alarms if any answer differs. On Serai's sample that is 595 orders, and 72 of them disagree.

LiveWhy the hour either side, not just the hour after3 min▶

Serai's drift only ever moves orders from 00:xx back to 23:xx, so checking the first hour would have been enough for this one bug. The check reads both sides of midnight anyway, 23:00 to 00:59 local, because the mirror bug exists: an offset that is one hour too far east moves 23:xx orders forward to the next day. A check shaped around the bug you already know finds only that bug; a check shaped around the risk, "the local day near its boundary", finds both directions.

The near-midnight set is chosen from legacy's local time, the side you trust, and every key in it must find a day on the new side. A key with no row on the new side counts as "another day" too: the build-along uses IS DISTINCT FROM so a missing day is a mismatch, not a NULL that quietly passes.

LiveThe edge check after legacy is switched off2 min▶

While legacy is loaded and read-only, the check compares two warehouses. Once the decommission log reaches its last step there is no legacy to ask. The check survives because the instant was never lost: recompute the day from the stored UTC instant with the zone's name, and compare it with the day the new warehouse stored. That is the "rule that encodes what legacy would have said" in Leader session 6. The build-along runs both versions on the same rows, and they find the same three orders.

Self-studyWhat the edge check cannot do2 min▶

The bench is honest about this: the edge check exists because someone knew to look for time zones. It is the cheapest possible check for one named risk and says nothing about the next one. The checks that find a drift nobody named are the exact daily comparison at rung 2, read by a person, and the parallel run's nightly diff from Builder session 7. A watch the week after should hold a few narrow checks like this one for risks you can name, and keep the daily view open for the ones you cannot.

Real world

The engines keep the instant; your SQL picks the day. PostgreSQL's documentation says a timestamptz is "stored internally as UTC, and the originally stated or assumed time zone is not retained". DuckDB's says its TIMESTAMPTZ values "are displayed with the UTC offset of the configured time zone". Both statements are why the edge check is possible at all, and why it pins the session time zone: the instant in storage is right on both sides, and every disagreement about the day comes from the conversion written in a query. Fix the conversion and the history is correct again, with no reload.

Part 3 · covers late data, caches and when the watch ends

Late rows, stale copies and the end of the watch

5 min live

Cutover moved the readers you listed through the reporting view. Two things did not move with them: a feed that keeps sending old orders, and any copy of the numbers that something took before the switch.

LiveThe late feed, now landing in new2 min▶

Orders delivered long after purchase keep arriving on the late feed, and after cutover they arrive in the new warehouse. On Serai's sample 367 orders were delivered more than 30 days after purchase. Builder session 6 showed the consequence of appending them: 8,981 rows from 8,614, and 367 duplicated keys. If the reload used a merge on key but the nightly feed job still appends, the drift comes back one night at a time. The watch item is one query, run nightly: any key that appears more than once. How to make the load itself safe to rerun belongs to the backfill sessions linked from session 6; the watch only checks that it stayed that way.

LiveThe copy that outlived the switch2 min▶

Session 8's pointer swap moved every reader that queries the reporting view. It cannot move a BI extract refreshed on its own schedule, a cache in front of a dashboard, a spreadsheet with a saved connection string, or a file a partner receives. Each of those either still points at legacy or holds numbers copied from it. There is no count for this on the bench and none is claimed here: the risk is real, and its size is whatever your estate has.

Detection is two questions per copy, asked by name: where does it read from, and when did it last refresh? A copy pointing at legacy is a missed switch. A copy pointing at new but last refreshed before cutover is still serving legacy numbers until its next refresh. The list comes from session 1's inventory plus the BI tool's own list of extracts, and the watch item closes only when every line on it is confirmed.

LiveWhen the watch ends2 min▶

Serai's end condition is the one in Leader session 6, word for word, because there must be one condition and not one per team. It is constructed policy: the rung-4 suite clean on 30 consecutive nights, with no duplicated key on any of them; the first month-end close signed inside the bar; and every cache and extract on the list confirmed as reading the new warehouse. Owner: the platform lead. When it is met, the sponsor signs step 2 of the decommission log and the rollback window closes.

Whether 30 nights is right is the same open question as the length of the parallel run: no source gives a number, and a business with a quarterly close might wait for that close instead. What the builder owns is that the condition is a query, not a feeling. The parallel run's 14-night rule in Builder session 7 and this 30-night rule are two different gates, written before each started.

Self-studyA watch is a comparison, not a monitor2 min▶

Every item above compares new against legacy, or against a rule that says what legacy would have said. When the watch ends, that comparison stops. Some checks are worth keeping in another form: the duplicated-key query becomes a uniqueness test on the new table, the edge check becomes a test on the conversion code. Turning those into lasting monitors with freshness, volume, schema and alert routing is the work of the Learn Data Observability course. Hand them over there by name in section 8, rather than letting them run against a legacy warehouse that is about to disappear.

Real world

Writes that landed on one side only. GitHub's analysis of its 21 October 2018 incident describes connectivity "restored in 43 seconds", after which a database "contained writes that were not present in the other data center". GitHub reports "degraded service for 24 hours and 11 minutes", having chosen to "prioritize data integrity over site usability and time to recovery." A warehouse cutover is not a failover, but the shape is the same: the dangerous rows are the ones that arrived somewhere after the moment everyone treats as the switch.

Build-along

Two conversions, one edge check

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You need DuckDB: pip install duckdb and a Python prompt, or the DuckDB command-line shell. Eight constructed orders, mostly in January 2017, inside the first Brazilian summer in Serai's sample. Legacy computed each order's day with the zone's name; new computed it with a fixed -03:00 and received one order twice from the late feed. Every output below is what DuckDB 1.5.3 printed on exactly these rows.

Load the constructed orders. Constructed Serai Commerce example. The session time zone is pinned to UTC so your own clock cannot leak in, and the ICU extension supplies named zones. In DuckDB 1.5.3 it loaded cleanly; if your build rejects the zone name, the two ICU lines are the fix. The last line loads o4 a second time, as the late feed would.

★ Setup (paste once)SET TimeZone = 'UTC'; INSTALL icu; LOAD icu; CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE orders_utc AS SELECT order_id, CAST(placed_utc AS TIMESTAMPTZ) AS placed_utc FROM (VALUES ('o1', '2017-01-14 02:30:00+00'), ('o2', '2017-01-14 01:40:00+00'), ('o3', '2017-01-14 15:00:00+00'), ('o4', '2017-01-15 02:05:00+00'), ('o5', '2017-01-15 03:10:00+00'), ('o6', '2017-01-31 02:20:00+00'), ('o7', '2017-07-10 03:30:00+00'), ('o8', '2019-11-20 02:30:00+00') ) t(order_id, placed_utc); CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE legacy_orders AS SELECT order_id, placed_utc, CAST(placed_utc AT TIME ZONE 'America/Sao_Paulo' AS DATE) AS order_day FROM orders_utc; CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE new_orders AS SELECT order_id, placed_utc, CAST(CAST(placed_utc AS TIMESTAMP) - INTERVAL 3 HOUR AS DATE) AS order_day FROM orders_utc; INSERT INTO new_orders SELECT * FROM new_orders WHERE order_id = 'o4'; SELECT count(*) AS legacy_rows, (SELECT count(*) FROM new_orders) AS new_rows FROM legacy_orders; -- legacy_rows 8 | new_rows 9

Q1, both conversions side by side. o1, o4 and o6 land on the day before under the fixed offset. o7 is in July, outside summer time, and o8 is from 2019, after Brazil stopped observing it: both rules agree on both. o5 is the odd one: the fixed offset puts it at 00:10, but its true local time is 01:10.

★ Q1 · named zone against fixed offsetSELECT order_id, strftime(placed_utc AT TIME ZONE 'America/Sao_Paulo', '%Y-%m-%d %H:%M') AS named_zone, strftime(CAST(placed_utc AS TIMESTAMP) - INTERVAL 3 HOUR, '%Y-%m-%d %H:%M') AS fixed_offset FROM orders_utc ORDER BY placed_utc; -- o2 | 2017-01-13 23:40 | 2017-01-13 22:40 -- o1 | 2017-01-14 00:30 | 2017-01-13 23:30 -- o3 | 2017-01-14 13:00 | 2017-01-14 12:00 -- o4 | 2017-01-15 00:05 | 2017-01-14 23:05 -- o5 | 2017-01-15 01:10 | 2017-01-15 00:10 -- o6 | 2017-01-31 00:20 | 2017-01-30 23:20 -- o7 | 2017-07-10 00:30 | 2017-07-10 00:30 -- o8 | 2019-11-19 23:30 | 2019-11-19 23:30

Q2, the drift at two grains. Orders per day and per month, legacy against new. New counts distinct keys here so the late duplicate does not muddy the time-zone question; Q5 handles it. Four of seven days differ and no month does. Look at 2017-01-14: o1 left it and o4 arrived, so its count matches while the orders in it do not. That is the cancellation behind 74 days for orders and 81 for item value on the bench.

★ Q2 · daily and monthly viewsSELECT 'daily' AS grain, count(*) AS buckets_compared, count(*) FILTER (WHERE coalesce(l.n,0) <> coalesce(n.n,0)) AS buckets_differ FROM (SELECT order_day AS d, count(*) AS n FROM legacy_orders GROUP BY 1) l FULL OUTER JOIN (SELECT order_day AS d, count(DISTINCT order_id) AS n FROM new_orders GROUP BY 1) n USING (d) UNION ALL SELECT 'monthly', count(*), count(*) FILTER (WHERE coalesce(l.n,0) <> coalesce(n.n,0)) FROM (SELECT date_trunc('month', order_day) AS m, count(*) AS n FROM legacy_orders GROUP BY 1) l FULL OUTER JOIN (SELECT date_trunc('month', order_day) AS m, count(DISTINCT order_id) AS n FROM new_orders GROUP BY 1) n USING (m); -- daily | 7 | 4 -- monthly | 3 | 0 SELECT strftime(d, '%Y-%m-%d') AS day, coalesce(l.n,0) AS legacy, coalesce(n.n,0) AS new FROM (SELECT order_day AS d, count(*) AS n FROM legacy_orders GROUP BY 1) l FULL OUTER JOIN (SELECT order_day AS d, count(DISTINCT order_id) AS n FROM new_orders GROUP BY 1) n USING (d) ORDER BY d; -- 2017-01-13 | 1 | 2 -- 2017-01-14 | 2 | 2 -- 2017-01-15 | 2 | 1 -- 2017-01-30 | 0 | 1 -- 2017-01-31 | 1 | 0 -- 2017-07-10 | 1 | 1 -- 2019-11-19 | 1 | 1

Q3, the edge check. Take every order placed in local hour 23 or 00 by legacy's clock, find the day new stored for the same key, and count disagreements. min(order_day) gives one day per key even when the late feed loaded it twice, and IS DISTINCT FROM counts a key missing from new as a mismatch.

★ Q3 · same order, same day?WITH near AS ( SELECT order_id, order_day AS legacy_day, hour(placed_utc AT TIME ZONE 'America/Sao_Paulo') AS local_hour FROM legacy_orders WHERE hour(placed_utc AT TIME ZONE 'America/Sao_Paulo') IN (23, 0) ), new_day AS ( SELECT order_id, min(order_day) AS new_day FROM new_orders GROUP BY order_id ) SELECT count(*) AS near_midnight, count(*) FILTER (WHERE n.new_day IS DISTINCT FROM near.legacy_day) AS on_another_day, string_agg(order_id, ', ' ORDER BY order_id) FILTER (WHERE n.new_day IS DISTINCT FROM near.legacy_day) AS which FROM near LEFT JOIN new_day n USING (order_id); -- near_midnight 6 | on_another_day 3 | o1, o4, o6

Q4, the same check with legacy switched off. No legacy table: recompute the day from the stored instant with the zone's name and compare it with what new stored. Same three orders. This is the version that keeps working through the decommission log.

★ Q4 · the rule instead of legacySELECT order_id, strftime(order_day, '%Y-%m-%d') AS stored_day, strftime(CAST(placed_utc AT TIME ZONE 'America/Sao_Paulo' AS DATE), '%Y-%m-%d') AS rule_day FROM (SELECT DISTINCT order_id, placed_utc, order_day FROM new_orders) WHERE hour(placed_utc AT TIME ZONE 'America/Sao_Paulo') IN (23, 0) AND order_day <> CAST(placed_utc AT TIME ZONE 'America/Sao_Paulo' AS DATE) ORDER BY order_id; -- o1 | 2017-01-13 | 2017-01-14 -- o4 | 2017-01-14 | 2017-01-15 -- o6 | 2017-01-30 | 2017-01-31

Q5, the late-feed watch. One query, run every night of the watch. Any row is an alarm; tonight o4 is there because the setup loaded it twice.

★ Q5 · keys that appear twiceSELECT order_id, count(*) AS copies FROM new_orders GROUP BY order_id HAVING count(*) > 1; -- o4 | 2

A watch that cannot end and cannot see -- week after: keep an eye on the dashboards for a while and -- raise anything that looks off. Monthly parity is green.

Note which clock picks the near-midnight set. Q3 and Q4 choose rows by the named zone's hour, never by the stored day or the fixed offset. Choose by the fixed offset and o5, at a true 01:10, joins the set while an order at a true 23:30 in summer would drop out of it: the check would be shaped by the bug it is meant to find.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Builder session 9 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Serai's signed monthly bar is green every month, yet the fixed-offset drift is planted. Why does rung 3 miss it?

The orders bar is exact, so looseness is not the cause. The drift never leaves its month in this sample, so the monthly grain cannot see it. Rung 4's edge check reads the days of the 595 near-midnight orders and finds 72 on another day.

2 · Legacy has been switched off. How do you keep running the edge check?

The instant is kept in storage; only the conversion was wrong. A named-zone recomputation encodes what legacy would have said. Orders since cutover cannot show the drift, because Brazil no longer observes summer time.

3 · A week after cutover the board pack still shows a legacy number, though the reporting view points at new. What is the most likely cause, and the check?

A pointer swap reaches only what reads through the pointer. Copies refreshed on their own schedule keep legacy numbers until they refresh or are repointed, which is why each one is a named watch item.
