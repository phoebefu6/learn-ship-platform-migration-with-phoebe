Part 1 · covers exact daily aggregates (rung 2)

Every metric, every day, exactly

5 min live

Compute the six metrics per day on each side with the same SQL, then demand that every value on every day is identical. On the bench that is 598 days of six numbers each. It is the most sensitive check on the ladder, and the most expensive to read.

LiveWhy exact by day finds everything3 min▶

A day is a small bucket, so almost any drift moves at least one number in it. On the bench the late rows change the order count on 205 of 598 days. The fixed offset moves 72 orders to the day before and changes the order count on 74 days (item value on 81). The missing review loaded as 0 shows on 63 days, the days that had an unreviewed order. Integer division changes items per order on 427 days; on the other days the true ratio is already a whole number, so truncating it changes nothing.

The case-sensitive join moves no aggregate at all, but rung 2 keeps the counts and keys from Builder session 4, so the key check still catches it. That is how rung 2 reaches 5 of 5 defects caught, 0 missed.

LiveThe price: two alarms nobody should act on2 min▶

The bench also plants two accepted differences, and exact daily flags both. Freight split equally across an order's lines and rounded per line differs on 80 days. Money summed as floating point differs in the last bits on 302 days of item value and 269 days of freight. That is 2 false alarms out of 3 chances (the two accepted differences and the no-drift control).

False alarms are not harmless. A report that is red every night for reasons everybody already knows teaches the team to stop reading it, and the next real defect arrives in a report nobody opens. Part 2 is how a suite keeps the sensitivity and loses the noise.

Self-studySame SQL on both sides, one long table3 min▶

Write the six metrics once as a query over each side's orders, identical text on both, and store that text in the runbook. Then turn the wide result (one row per day, six columns) into a long one (one row per day and metric) with UNPIVOT. A long table lets one full outer join on day and metric compare all six metrics at once, and a day that exists on only one side shows up as a row with a NULL partner instead of vanishing.

Compare with IS DISTINCT FROM, not <>. PostgreSQL's documentation puts it plainly: "Do not write expression = NULL because NULL is not 'equal to' NULL". A day missing on one side makes one value NULL, and <> would quietly drop exactly the row you need. The build-along does both.

Real world

The tools compute the aggregates; the grain is yours. Google Cloud's migration overview tells you to "run the data validation tool to validate that your source and destination tables match." The tool's open-source README lists column validation with "count, sum, avg, min, max, stddev, group by", beside row, schema and custom-query validation. Read both as vendor claims about Google's own tooling, and re-verify the README before you rely on it. "Group by" is the important word: the tool will group by whatever you name. Which column, which grain and which tolerance are decisions, and they belong in the runbook, not in a default.

Part 2 · covers tolerances by metric at the monthly grain (rung 3)

A tolerance per metric, at the month

5 min live

Rung 3 replaces exact days with the grain Serai's board reads, the month (21 of them in the sample), and gives each metric its own tolerance, signed before anyone looked. The two false alarms disappear. One defect goes with them.

LiveWhy a count is exact and money is relative3 min▶

Orders and reviewed orders are counts. Nothing in a count rounds, so any gap is a defect and the tolerance is zero. Item value and freight are sums of money computed by two different engines, so they can differ in the last bits, and the gap grows with the size of the month, so their tolerance is relative: 0.05% of the legacy value. Items per order and average review are ratios on a fixed scale, so their tolerance is absolute, on that scale: 0.005 items and 0.01 of a 1 to 5 score.

Then check each band against the bench. The floating-point gaps are about 2e-10 BRL in a month and the per-line rounding moves the whole sample's freight from BRL 195,579.02 to BRL 195,579.59; both sit inside 0.05% every month. The defects sit outside: the late rows fail orders in all 21 months, integer division fails items per order in every month, and the missing review as 0 fails reviewed orders in 20 months and the average in 19. Rung 3: 4 of 5 caught, 0 false alarms.

LiveWhat the month hides2 min▶

The fixed offset moves 72 orders from just after local midnight to the day before. None of them crosses a month end in this sample, so every monthly total is unchanged and 0 months fail. A monthly bar does not see a drift whose whole effect is inside the month. That is the one defect rung 3 misses.

Serai's ops dashboard reads some numbers daily, so the month is not the only grain people read. Going back to exact days would bring the two false alarms back. The ladder's answer is rung 4, a targeted check on the 595 orders placed within an hour of local midnight, which Builder session 9 builds. Tonight, name the gap in the runbook: "the monthly bar does not see shifts inside a month; the edge check covers time zones."

Self-studyRecompute each grain from rows3 min▶

It is tempting to build the monthly numbers by rolling up the daily ones. For sums and counts that works. For a ratio it does not: the average of daily averages weights a quiet day like a busy one. On tonight's legacy side, February's review scores give a different answer each way:

★ Run after the build-along setup and viewsSELECT (SELECT avg(avg_review) FROM legacy_daily WHERE day >= DATE '2017-02-01') AS mean_of_daily_means, (SELECT avg_review FROM legacy_monthly WHERE month = DATE '2017-02-01') AS monthly_from_rows; -- mean_of_daily_means 4.333333333333333 | monthly_from_rows 4.0

Compute every grain from the rows, with the same SQL on both sides, and the comparison stays honest. A parity check that compares two wrong numbers can pass while both disagree with what the board sees.

Self-studyBars that are too loose, and the contested question2 min▶

A tolerance chosen for comfort hides any defect smaller than itself. With 3% on every metric, the missing review as 0 never fails a month: its largest monthly gap is 2.22%, in February 2017. Builder session 10 lets you press that lever and watch the score fall; Leader session 4 is where the signer learns to refuse it.

Whether aggregates are "enough", or row hashing is needed too, is genuinely contested, and Builder session 4 laid out the trade. Aggregates compare at the grain people read and need no key; they can average away a single changed row. Which miss is more expensive is a business call for whoever signs the bar.

Real world

A definition of done, written before the comparison. Google's 2019 blog on migrating data warehouses, a vendor's own guidance, says: "At the end of each iteration, validate that the use case was successfully migrated according to your definition of done". For a number people read, the definition of done is the signed bar: metric, grain, tolerance, what it absorbs, what it must still catch. Signed first, so that a result can never move it.

Part 3 · covers floating point and rounding

Money that differs in the last bits

5 min live

The two accepted differences on the bench are not mistakes anyone will fix. They are how numbers behave when two engines store and add them differently. Run them yourself once and you will recognise them in any diff.

LiveRun a sum that differs3 min▶

A DOUBLE stores 10.10 as the nearest binary fraction, not 10.10, so sums drift in the last bits; a DECIMAL stores cents exactly. And because floating-point addition is not associative, the order rows are added in changes the result. Two engines that split a sum across workers differently can disagree on the same rows. Paste this into DuckDB:

★ Float against DECIMAL, and the order of additionSELECT sum(CAST(v AS DOUBLE)) AS as_double, sum(CAST(v AS DECIMAL(10,2))) AS as_decimal FROM (VALUES (10.10), (20.20)) t(v); -- as_double 30.299999999999997 | as_decimal 30.30 SELECT (0.1::DOUBLE + 0.2::DOUBLE) + 0.3::DOUBLE AS left_first, 0.1::DOUBLE + (0.2::DOUBLE + 0.3::DOUBLE) AS right_first; -- left_first 0.6000000000000001 | right_first 0.6

Do not assume you can predict which values drift: in the same DuckDB, sum(CAST(0.1 AS DOUBLE)) over ten rows printed exactly 1.0. Test on your own rows. The professional rule is simple: store money as DECIMAL or integer cents on the new side if you can; if either side sums in floating point, sign a small relative tolerance for money and write "floating point" in its absorbs column.

LiveRound once, at the grain you report2 min▶

When an order's freight is split across its lines and each line is rounded to cents, the lines no longer add back to the order. Sometimes the total loses a cent, sometimes it gains one:

★ Per-line rounding, and the remainder fixSELECT freight, CAST(freight / 3 AS DECIMAL(10,2)) AS per_line, CAST(freight / 3 AS DECIMAL(10,2)) * 3 AS three_lines FROM (VALUES (10.00::DECIMAL(10,2)), (20.00::DECIMAL(10,2))) f(freight); -- 10.00 | 3.33 | 9.99 -- 20.00 | 6.67 | 20.01 SELECT line_no, CASE WHEN line_no < 3 THEN CAST(10.00 / 3 AS DECIMAL(10,2)) ELSE 10.00 - 2 * CAST(10.00 / 3 AS DECIMAL(10,2)) END AS line_freight FROM range(1, 4) r(line_no); -- 1 | 3.33 -- 2 | 3.33 -- 3 | 3.34

On the bench, 90 orders are split this way and the whole sample's freight moves by BRL 0.57. If the new model needs freight per line, give the last line the remainder so the order still adds up; if it was built that way already and the business accepts it, sign it as an accepted difference with a tolerance that absorbs it and still catches the late rows.

Self-studyAccepted is a signature, not a shrug2 min▶

An accepted difference needs three things in the runbook: what it is (money summed as floating point), how big it can be on the bench or in the parallel run (about 2e-10 BRL a month), and the tolerance that absorbs it while still catching the defect it must never hide. Without the third, "accepted" quietly grows to cover whatever shows up next.

Never accept a difference because it is small today. Accept it because you know its mechanism and can say why it cannot grow. Floating point qualifies. "Freight is a few cents out" without a mechanism does not.

Real world

One symbol, two numbers. PostgreSQL's documentation says integer division "truncates the result towards zero". DuckDB's says "When both operands are integers, / performs floating points division (5 / 2 = 2.5)", and "// performs integer division (5 // 2 = 2)". Both are engine documentation, read at source. The same sum(items) / count(*) therefore means two things on two engines, which is how items per order became 1 on the bench. Builder session 3 wrote the translation rule; tonight's daily check is the proof that it held, on 427 days.

Build-along

Six metrics, compared by day and by month

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You need DuckDB: pip install duckdb and a Python prompt, or the DuckDB command-line shell. Constructed Serai Commerce example: ten orders in January and February 2017, during Brazilian summer time. Legacy stores money as DECIMAL; new stores it as DOUBLE and carries three more planted faults. Every row is present on both sides, so Builder session 4's key report would pass. Paste the setup once, then run the queries in order. Every output below is what DuckDB 1.5.3 printed on this exact pair.

Load the constructed pair and the bar. Legacy holds ten orders with money as DECIMAL. New holds the same ten orders with money as DOUBLE and three planted faults: o4, placed at 00:30 local time on 16 January, landed on the 15th under the fixed offset; o6's missing review score was loaded as 0; o7's freight of 10.00 was split over three lines and rounded per line to 9.99. The bar table is Serai's signed bar, constructed policy.

★ Setup (paste once)CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE legacy_orders AS SELECT order_id, CAST(order_day AS DATE) AS order_day, items, CAST(item_value AS DECIMAL(10,2)) AS item_value, CAST(freight AS DECIMAL(10,2)) AS freight, CAST(review_score AS INTEGER) AS review_score FROM (VALUES ('o1','2017-01-14',1, 10.10,15.10,5), ('o2','2017-01-14',2, 20.20,20.00,4), ('o3','2017-01-16',1, 35.00,12.40,5), ('o4','2017-01-16',1, 64.00,18.30,3), ('o5','2017-01-20',3, 99.00,21.50,4), ('o6','2017-02-03',1, 44.00,11.20,NULL), ('o7','2017-02-03',3, 72.40,10.00,5), ('o8','2017-02-07',2, 28.90,16.60,2), ('o9','2017-02-07',1,130.00,19.90,4), ('o10','2017-02-10',1,59.90,14.00,5) ) t(order_id, order_day, items, item_value, freight, review_score); CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE new_orders AS SELECT order_id, CAST(order_day AS DATE) AS order_day, items, CAST(item_value AS DOUBLE) AS item_value, CAST(freight AS DOUBLE) AS freight, CAST(review_score AS INTEGER) AS review_score FROM (VALUES ('o1','2017-01-14',1, 10.10,15.10,5), ('o2','2017-01-14',2, 20.20,20.00,4), ('o3','2017-01-16',1, 35.00,12.40,5), ('o4','2017-01-15',1, 64.00,18.30,3), ('o5','2017-01-20',3, 99.00,21.50,4), ('o6','2017-02-03',1, 44.00,11.20,0), ('o7','2017-02-03',3, 72.40, 9.99,5), ('o8','2017-02-07',2, 28.90,16.60,2), ('o9','2017-02-07',1,130.00,19.90,4), ('o10','2017-02-10',1,59.90,14.00,5) ) t(order_id, order_day, items, item_value, freight, review_score); CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE bar AS SELECT * FROM (VALUES ('orders','abs',0.0), ('item_value','rel',0.0005), ('freight','rel',0.0005), ('items_per_order','abs',0.005), ('reviewed','abs',0.0), ('avg_review','abs',0.01) ) b(metric, kind, tol);

Q1, the six metrics per day, same SQL on both sides. Each view casts to DOUBLE only at the end, so the comparison has one type; legacy's DECIMAL sums are exact before that cast. New has one more day than legacy: the day o4 moved to.

★ Q1 · daily viewsCREATE OR REPLACE VIEW legacy_daily AS SELECT order_day AS day, count(*)::DOUBLE AS orders, sum(item_value)::DOUBLE AS item_value, sum(freight)::DOUBLE AS freight, sum(items) / count(*) AS items_per_order, count(review_score)::DOUBLE AS reviewed, avg(review_score) AS avg_review FROM legacy_orders GROUP BY order_day; CREATE OR REPLACE VIEW new_daily AS SELECT order_day AS day, count(*)::DOUBLE AS orders, sum(item_value)::DOUBLE AS item_value, sum(freight)::DOUBLE AS freight, sum(items) / count(*) AS items_per_order, count(review_score)::DOUBLE AS reviewed, avg(review_score) AS avg_review FROM new_orders GROUP BY order_day; SELECT (SELECT count(*) FROM legacy_daily) AS legacy_days, (SELECT count(*) FROM new_daily) AS new_days; -- legacy_days 6 | new_days 7

Q3, rung 3: the same six metrics per month, against the bar. Recompute the month from rows, never from the daily views. Each gap is measured the way its metric is signed: relative for money, absolute for the rest. January passes everything, although o4 sits on the wrong day inside it.

Q4, the failing-buckets report: runbook section 4, rungs 2 and 3. One row per failing day or month, with the metrics that failed it. This is the output a person reads every night of the parallel run, so it lists buckets, not fifteen loose cells.

★ Q4 · failing bucketsWITH dl AS (UNPIVOT legacy_daily ON COLUMNS(* EXCLUDE (day)) INTO NAME metric VALUE v), dr AS (UNPIVOT new_daily ON COLUMNS(* EXCLUDE (day)) INTO NAME metric VALUE v), ml AS (UNPIVOT legacy_monthly ON COLUMNS(* EXCLUDE (month)) INTO NAME metric VALUE v), mr AS (UNPIVOT new_monthly ON COLUMNS(* EXCLUDE (month)) INTO NAME metric VALUE v), daily_fail AS ( SELECT 'daily exact' AS check_name, strftime(coalesce(dl.day, dr.day), '%Y-%m-%d') AS bucket, coalesce(dl.metric, dr.metric) AS metric FROM dl FULL OUTER JOIN dr ON dl.day = dr.day AND dl.metric = dr.metric WHERE dl.v IS DISTINCT FROM dr.v), monthly_fail AS ( SELECT 'monthly bar' AS check_name, strftime(coalesce(ml.month, mr.month), '%Y-%m') AS bucket, coalesce(ml.metric, mr.metric) AS metric FROM ml FULL OUTER JOIN mr ON ml.month = mr.month AND ml.metric = mr.metric JOIN bar b ON b.metric = coalesce(ml.metric, mr.metric) WHERE ml.v IS NULL OR mr.v IS NULL OR (CASE b.kind WHEN 'rel' THEN abs(mr.v - ml.v) / abs(ml.v) ELSE abs(mr.v - ml.v) END) > b.tol) SELECT check_name, bucket, count(*) AS metrics_failing, string_agg(metric, ', ' ORDER BY metric) AS which FROM (SELECT * FROM daily_fail UNION ALL SELECT * FROM monthly_fail) GROUP BY check_name, bucket ORDER BY check_name, bucket; -- daily exact | 2017-01-14 | 1 | item_value -- daily exact | 2017-01-15 | 6 | avg_review, freight, item_value, items_per_order, orders, reviewed -- daily exact | 2017-01-16 | 5 | avg_review, freight, item_value, orders, reviewed -- daily exact | 2017-02-03 | 3 | avg_review, freight, reviewed -- monthly bar | 2017-02 | 2 | avg_review, reviewed

Q5, classify every failing bucket by hand. No query does this part. For each row of Q4, write the cause and whether it is a defect or an accepted difference. Tonight's answer: 2017-01-14 is floating point (accepted, a false alarm at rung 2); 2017-01-15 and 2017-01-16 are the offset (a defect, caught only by daily exact); 2017-02-03 is the review as 0 (a defect) plus per-line rounding (accepted); the February month is the review as 0 again, with the rounding absorbed by the bar. The pair reproduces the bench's trade in miniature: daily exact catches both defects with a false alarm, the monthly bar is quiet and misses the offset.

Why both grains stay in the suite. The monthly bar is the one Serai signs, because it is quiet and it is the grain the board reads. The daily exact report is still worth keeping as a read-only view during the parallel run: it is the check most likely to surface a drift nobody planned for. Nothing here checks the time-zone edge directly. That is rung 4, the edge check of Builder session 9, and it is why Serai's suite does not stop at rung 3.

Three questions

Clear all three and Builder session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the bench, rung 2 (exact daily aggregates) catches 5 of 5 defects. What are its 2 false alarms?

Both are differences Serai accepted in advance. Exact equality flags any gap, including the last-bit gaps of floating point and the cents moved by per-line rounding.

2 · In tonight's build-along, why does January pass the monthly bar although o4 sits on the wrong day?

Orders are compared exactly, and the monthly views are computed from rows. The drift is invisible at the month because its whole effect is inside the month, the same reason the bench's rung 3 misses the 72 shifted orders.

3 · Why does Serai's bar compare reviewed orders exactly but item value within 0.05%?

Each tolerance is set by how the metric is computed and written next to the drift it absorbs and the drift it must still catch. 0.05% clears floating point and per-line rounding and still catches the late rows.
