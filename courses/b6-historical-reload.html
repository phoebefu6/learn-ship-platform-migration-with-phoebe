Part 1 · covers the reload as one cutover step

History moves one month at a time

5 min live

The reload is the biggest single load the new warehouse will ever take, and it happens before anyone reads from it. Cut it into windows small enough that a failure points at one place, and prove each window before the next one starts.

LiveWhy a window, and why a month3 min▶

A reload of everything in one go has one verdict: it matches or it does not. When it does not, you have 21 months of history and no idea where to look. Cut it into windows and each parity run covers one window, so a failure names a month, and redoing it means one month of load instead of all of it.

The window follows the grain the parity bar is signed at. Serai's bar is monthly, its sample holds 21 months, so the reload has 21 windows and each window ends in the same monthly check the business signed. If your bar were daily, a daily window would make the reload's failures speak the same language as the bar. A table too big for a month at a time can be cut finer; the rule is that every window must end at a boundary the parity run can compare.

Self-studyWhere reload ends and backfill begins2 min▶

Mechanically a reload is a backfill: run the load for a past period. The craft of making that safe, so a window can be rerun without doubling anything, is idempotency, and it has an owner. Read the Data Reliability builder session on backfills, its leader session on backfills and debt, and the Data Orchestration session on backfills. Those pages own how to rerun a window safely.

This page owns what is particular to a migration: the reload is a step of the cutover with a gate after every window, the comparison is between two warehouses rather than within one, and there is a second feed, the late-row feed, that keeps writing into the target while the reload runs. That feed is Part 2.

Real world

The vendor guides gate every iteration. Google Cloud's 2019 migration blog asks that "At the end of each iteration, validate that the use case was successfully migrated according to your definition of done". Its documentation for the move itself says to "run the data validation tool to validate that your source and destination tables match". Read both as vendor claims about their own process. The pattern still holds on any engine: a window is not loaded until a comparison against the source says so.

Part 2 · covers the late-row feed and the load rule

The late feed sends some orders twice

5 min live

Some orders are delivered long after they are bought. On the bench, 367 orders were delivered more than 30 days after purchase, and Serai's late-row feed sends each of them again when it finally lands. Whether the new warehouse holds 8,614 rows or 8,981 comes down to one word in the load: append or merge.

LiveWhy the feed exists, and why legacy never doubled3 min▶

A late-row feed is ordinary plumbing: the nightly load picks up orders as they are bought, and a second feed picks up the ones whose delivery came in long after, so the delivery date and anything that depends on it are filled in. The legacy load merged that feed on the order key, quietly, years ago. Nobody wrote the rule down because nothing ever broke.

The new load was written from the job's description: "load the late feed into orders". Written as an append, it adds every late order a second time. On the bench, 367 orders were delivered more than 30 days after purchase; each one comes back, so the table holds 8,981 rows instead of 8,614, and item value moves from BRL 1,197,842.24 to BRL 1,262,425.64. Every job reports success. The rule belongs in runbook section 5 as SQL, not as a sentence: the late feed merges on the order key; it never appends.

Self-studyA key is what makes the merge possible3 min▶

A merge needs to know that two rows are the same order, and only a key says so. Give the target a primary key and two things change. A plain insert of a duplicate stops being silent: DuckDB refuses it with a constraint error naming the key, as the build-along shows. And the merge becomes one statement, INSERT ... ON CONFLICT (order_id) DO UPDATE, which updates the row that is already there instead of adding another.

Not every engine enforces a declared primary key, so the refusal you see in DuckDB may not happen on your target; check what it does before you rely on it. Where keys are not enforced, the merge statement your engine offers is the rule, and the duplicated-key check in Part 3 is the only thing that will notice when someone writes an append instead.

Appends: duplicates every late orderINSERT INTO orders SELECT * FROM late_feed;
★ Merges on the key: one row per orderINSERT INTO orders SELECT * FROM late_feed ON CONFLICT (order_id) DO UPDATE SET delivered_on = EXCLUDED.delivered_on, item_value = EXCLUDED.item_value;
Real world

Validation needs the key too. AWS's Database Migration Service documentation says its validation "compares each row in the source with its corresponding row at the target", and that "Data validation requires that the table has a primary key or unique index." That is a vendor claim about one tool, and it describes a general fact: without a key, neither the load nor the check can tell a late order from a new one. Decide the key before the reload, not after the first duplicate.

Part 3 · covers the check after each window

Reload, then prove it

5 min live

The late-row drift is the loudest of the seven on the bench: every rung of the ladder catches it. But only one rung tells you which rows to fix, and that is the one to put first in the gate after each window.

LiveThe gate after each window3 min▶

Run the suite you already built, scoped to the window just loaded: counts, then keys both ways with duplicates, then the monthly bar. On the bench, with the late rows appended, rung 0 already fails (8,614 against 8,981) and rung 1 fails with 367 duplicated keys, exactly the difference the count saw, now with names. The daily view fails on 205 of 598 days and the monthly bar fails orders in all 21 months.

Order the gate so the first failure is the most useful one. A count tells you the window is wrong; the duplicated-key list tells you which orders, which is the input to the fix. The aggregates come next because they catch what keys cannot: a value that changed while every key stayed put, which is what sessions 3 and 5 were about.

Self-studyIs a row check enough, or do you need aggregates too?3 min▶

Practitioners disagree, and the tools reflect it. Google's open-source Data Validation Tool offers both column validation (count, sum, avg, min, max, stddev, group by) and row validation with SHA256 row hashing. AWS DMS goes row by row and needs a key to do it. Both are vendor claims about their own tools. A row hash catches a changed value on a single order that an aggregate could average away; an aggregate is cheaper and speaks the grain the business signed.

Neither is "enough" in general. The answer depends on what a missed row costs, which is a business call: a refund ledger and a marketing dashboard deserve different gates. Write down which one this reload uses per table, and who agreed to it, in runbook section 5.

Real world

Rows that exist on one side only. In GitHub's 21 October 2018 incident, connectivity was "restored in 43 seconds", but the database then "contained writes that were not present in the other data center". The team chose to "prioritize data integrity over site usability and time to recovery", and ran degraded for 24 hours and 11 minutes while it reconciled. A reload is calmer than an outage, and the lesson carries: reconciling rows by key is slow work, so the gate that finds duplicates should run after every window, not once at the end.

Build-along

Two windows, one late feed, two load rules

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You need DuckDB: pip install duckdb and a Python prompt, or the DuckDB command-line shell. Paste the setup once, then run each step in order in the same session. Every output below is what DuckDB printed on this exact data. Constructed Serai Commerce example: eight orders in two monthly windows, two of them delivered more than 30 days after purchase.

Load the constructed orders and two empty targets. legacy_orders is the source of truth, one row per order. late_feed is every order delivered more than 30 days after purchase, which the feed will send again. new_append has no key; new_merge has a primary key on order_id.

★ Setup (paste once)CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE legacy_orders AS SELECT order_id, CAST(purchased_on AS DATE) AS purchased_on, CAST(delivered_on AS DATE) AS delivered_on, CAST(item_value AS DECIMAL(10,2)) AS item_value FROM (VALUES ('o101','2017-01-05','2017-01-12',129.90), ('o102','2017-01-09','2017-02-20', 59.00), ('o103','2017-01-17','2017-01-25',210.50), ('o104','2017-01-28','2017-02-03', 45.00), ('o105','2017-02-02','2017-02-10', 88.80), ('o106','2017-02-11','2017-03-30',150.00), ('o107','2017-02-19','2017-02-27', 32.40), ('o108','2017-02-25','2017-03-06', 76.00) ) t(order_id, purchased_on, delivered_on, item_value); CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE late_feed AS SELECT * FROM legacy_orders WHERE delivered_on - purchased_on > 30; CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE new_append (order_id VARCHAR, purchased_on DATE, delivered_on DATE, item_value DECIMAL(10,2)); CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE new_merge (order_id VARCHAR PRIMARY KEY, purchased_on DATE, delivered_on DATE, item_value DECIMAL(10,2));

Q1, what the late feed holds. Two orders, each already in legacy once. The same rule on the bench selects 367 of 8,614 orders.

Q2, reload the first window into both targets. One month, by purchase date. The same rows go into both tables.

★ Q2 · window 2017-01INSERT INTO new_append SELECT * FROM legacy_orders WHERE date_trunc('month', purchased_on) = DATE '2017-01-01'; INSERT INTO new_merge SELECT * FROM legacy_orders WHERE date_trunc('month', purchased_on) = DATE '2017-01-01';

Q3, the parity run for each window. Rows, duplicated keys and item value per month, legacy against new, with a verdict. A month not yet loaded says so instead of failing. This is the gate: January passes, so February may start.

★ Q3 · window parity on new_appendWITH l AS ( SELECT date_trunc('month', purchased_on) AS win, count(*) AS legacy_rows, sum(item_value) AS legacy_value FROM legacy_orders GROUP BY win), n AS ( SELECT date_trunc('month', purchased_on) AS win, count(*) AS new_rows, count(*) - count(DISTINCT order_id) AS dup_keys, sum(item_value) AS new_value FROM new_append GROUP BY win) SELECT strftime(win, '%Y-%m') AS month, legacy_rows, new_rows, dup_keys, legacy_value, new_value, CASE WHEN new_rows IS NULL THEN 'not loaded' WHEN legacy_rows = new_rows AND dup_keys = 0 AND legacy_value = new_value THEN 'pass' ELSE 'fail' END AS verdict FROM l FULL OUTER JOIN n USING (win) ORDER BY month; -- 2017-01 | 4 | 4 | 0 | 444.40 | 444.40 | pass -- 2017-02 | 4 | NULL | NULL | 347.20 | NULL | not loaded

Reload the second window and run Q3 again. Repeat Q2 with DATE '2017-02-01' in both statements, then rerun Q3 unchanged. Both windows pass on new_append, and the same holds on new_merge. So far the two rules are indistinguishable.

Q4, the late feed arrives. Append it to the keyless table and count: ten rows, eight keys, and no error. Then try the same plain insert on the keyed table, and finally the merge.

★ Q4a · append into new_appendINSERT INTO new_append SELECT * FROM late_feed; SELECT count(*) AS rows, count(DISTINCT order_id) AS keys FROM new_append; -- 10 | 8
Q4b · plain insert into new_merge: refusedINSERT INTO new_merge SELECT * FROM late_feed; -- Constraint Error: Duplicate key "order_id: o102" violates primary key constraint.
★ Q4c · merge into new_mergeINSERT INTO new_merge SELECT * FROM late_feed ON CONFLICT (order_id) DO UPDATE SET delivered_on = EXCLUDED.delivered_on, item_value = EXCLUDED.item_value; SELECT count(*) AS rows, count(DISTINCT order_id) AS keys FROM new_merge; -- 8 | 8

Rerun Q3 on both targets. As written it checks new_append; change FROM new_append to FROM new_merge for the second run. The appended table fails both windows, one extra row and one duplicated key each; the merged table passes both.

★ Q3 after the late feed-- new_append -- 2017-01 | 4 | 5 | 1 | 444.40 | 503.40 | fail -- 2017-02 | 4 | 5 | 1 | 347.20 | 497.20 | fail -- new_merge -- 2017-01 | 4 | 4 | 0 | 444.40 | 444.40 | pass -- 2017-02 | 4 | 4 | 0 | 347.20 | 347.20 | pass

Q5, the fix list. The duplicated keys by name, with how many copies. On a real reload this is what the window's owner works from; on the bench it is 367 keys long.

★ Q5 · duplicated keys on new_appendSELECT order_id, count(*) AS copies FROM new_append GROUP BY order_id HAVING count(*) > 1 ORDER BY order_id; -- o102 | 2 -- o106 | 2

Notice when the parity run went red. Both windows passed right after the reload and failed only after the late feed ran. On a real migration the late feed keeps running for the whole reload and the parallel run, so a window that passed on Monday can fail on Thursday. Run the gate again after every late-feed load, not only after the window's own load. Builder session 7 turns that into a nightly diff.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Builder session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the bench the late feed is appended instead of merged. How many rows does the new warehouse hold?

Appending adds every late order again, so 8,614 plus 367 makes 8,981. Merging on the order key keeps 8,614. Every job reports success either way, which is why the rule has to be written as SQL.

2 · Why reload Serai's history in monthly windows rather than all at once?

The window follows the grain of the signed bar. A failure then points at one month, every earlier window is already proved, and the redo is one month of load instead of all 21.

3 · Every rung of the ladder catches the late rows. Which check should come first in the gate after a window, and why?

All of them see the drift; only the key check gives you the fix list. The aggregates still run after it, because they catch values that change while every key stays put.
