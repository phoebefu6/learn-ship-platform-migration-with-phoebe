Ship It: Data Platform Migration · Leader session 3 of 6

Sequence and scope: lift, rebuild or retire

An inventory says what you own and who reads it. It does not say how each table moves, or in what order. Tonight every table on Serai Commerce's list gets one call: lift it as it is, rebuild it for the new warehouse, or retire it. Then the move gets a shape, and here the evidence runs out: one big switch or a phased move with a parallel run is a real disagreement, and you will hear both sides. You leave with a scope sheet a steering group can sign: a decision, a reason and a wave for every table.

Part 1 · covers one decision per asset and the criteria behind it

One call per table

6 min live

Every table in scope gets exactly one of three answers. Lift: translate it as it is, so it produces the same numbers in the new dialect. Rebuild: redesign it for the new warehouse, because a straight copy would carry a fault or cost more than it saves. Retire: switch it off before the move. Two questions, asked in order, decide which.

LiveThree answers, and what each one costs3 min▶
AnswerWhat happens to the tableWhat it costs laterWhat parity means for it
LiftThe same logic, translated into the new dialect, with the translation rules from Builder session 3 appliedA translation, a historical reload, a parity proofEvery number the same as legacy, or explained
RebuildNew design for the new warehouse: a key where there was none, a merge where there was an append, a named time zone where there was an offsetEverything a lift costs, plus design and review timeStill the same as legacy, unless a difference was written on the done sheet before anyone looked
RetireStopped and revoked with a date, then archived and dropped, as session 2 set outA conversation and a dateNone: nothing to prove

Read the last column twice. A rebuild is not a licence to change numbers. Serai rebuilds stg.orders and mart.orders_daily so that they give the same answers as legacy through a better design; the readers should see no change at all. If a rebuild is also meant to fix something legacy got wrong, that intended difference goes on the done sheet as "explained", with an owner, before the first parity run. Otherwise every difference it makes looks like a defect, and every defect looks like a design choice.

LiveThe two questions, applied to Serai3 min▶

Question 1, does anyone read it, or feed what is read? Session 2's inventory already answered this: 5 tables are read by people, 6 only by jobs that build those 5, and 3 by nobody. The 3 retire candidates leave here and never reach question 2.

Question 2, is its SQL complex, or does it carry a drift, and does the fix change its design? Session 1 named the seven drifts. Some are fixed by a rule: Export C's lower-case customer keys are normalised on load, and a missing review score stays NULL instead of becoming 0. Those tables are lifts with a rule. Two are not. stg.orders receives the late-row feed, and the only safe fix is to merge on the order key instead of appending, which means giving the table a key and a different load. mart.orders_daily turns timestamps into local days, and the fix for the fixed -03:00 offset is a named time zone with its daylight-saving history, which changes how the table is built. Both are rebuilds.

The mapping of drifts to tables is constructed for the case. On your estate, the builders' translation rules and their lineage tell you which table carries which fault.

Self-studyWhere scope quietly grows3 min▶

Once a team is redesigning one table, the next one always looks worth redesigning too. Each extra rebuild adds design and review time, and, worse, each one is a table whose numbers someone may be tempted to "improve" on the way through. A move that is also a redesign has two reasons for every difference, and a parity check cannot tell them apart.

Three habits keep the rebuild column short. Rebuild only for a reason on question 2, written in one line on the scope sheet. Put every intended change of a number on the done sheet before parity runs. And park every other improvement on a list for after cutover, when legacy is off and a difference can only have one cause.

Real world

Vendors put a decision step before the move. A 2019 Google Cloud blog on migrating data warehouses lays out its phases as "Prepare and discover", "Assess and plan" and "Execute", and asks that "At the end of each iteration, validate that the use case was successfully migrated according to your definition of done". Read it as a vendor describing the process around its own product, never as a recommendation of that product. The per-table call is what "assess and plan" means in practice, and the definition of done it points to is the sheet you built in session 1.

Part 2 · covers big bang against a phased move with a parallel run

One switch or many: a live disagreement

6 min live

Once you know what moves, you choose the shape of the move. Build everything, prove it as a whole and move every reader on one day; or move in waves, each running beside legacy until it is proved, and move readers wave by wave. Both have serious advocates, and no published evidence says which fails less often.

LiveThe case for phased waves with a parallel run2 min▶

Each wave is small, so when it fails parity the cause is in a handful of tables, not anywhere in the estate. Readers move a few at a time, so a wrong number reaches one team, not the board pack and the finance close on the same morning. Legacy keeps loading throughout, so every night there are two answers to compare, and the way back for a wave is to point its readers at legacy again.

The vendor guides argue for this shape. The 2019 Google Cloud blog describes an iterative move in which "both your data warehouse and BigQuery can be actively used in parallel". That is a vendor claim about its own process, and a vendor has a reason to prefer a move that starts small and starts soon.

LiveThe case for one switch2 min▶

A phased move means two warehouses, both loaded, both paid for, both watched, for as long as the waves take. Some wave tables are read by things that have not moved, so data has to be fed across from one system to the other, and every one of those feeds is one more thing to build, test and later switch off. Readers who use tables from several waves see two systems at once. A single switch avoids all of that: build the new warehouse whole, prove it whole, and move every reader together.

Its risk is concentration. Everything that was not caught in testing arrives on the same day, for every reader, with the whole estate as the search space. The Bank of England fined TSB "a total of £48,650,000" in 2022, finding it "failed to organise and control the IT migration programme adequately" after its 2018 platform migration, and TSB's own release says go-live "followed a long period of testing, with reports to the Board signalling readiness." That is one bank, one programme, and a migration of customer systems rather than a warehouse. It shows the cost of a large switch that goes wrong. It does not show how often one does.

Self-studyWhat decides it for your estate3 min▶

Because nobody has published how often either shape fails, the choice is a judgement about your estate, and the scope sheet should record it as one: who chose, on what reasons, on what date. Questions worth writing answers to:

  • Can both warehouses be fed from the same sources every night, or would a phased move need feeds between the two?
  • How tangled are the readers? A dashboard that reads tables from three waves cannot move until the last of them has.
  • How long can you afford to run two systems? No source gives a length for a parallel run; Serai's 4 weeks is constructed, and Builder session 7 takes the question up.
  • What is the way back? Session 5 writes the rollback; a phased move has one per wave, a single switch has one for everything.

Many teams land in between: build and prove in waves, then move readers in one or two planned switches. Even a team that chooses one switch still needs the wave order from Part 3, because it decides what is built and proved first.

Real world

A fine that names the programme, not the code. The Bank of England's December 2022 release on TSB does not blame one faulty table or one bad line of SQL. It finds the bank "failed to organise and control the IT migration programme adequately". Whatever shape you choose, the finding is about the choosing: who decided, on what evidence, and who could stop it. Write the shape decision into the runbook with a name next to it, so that it is a decision someone owns rather than a default nobody chose.

Part 3 · covers the order of moves, from retirements to reports

The graph sets the order, the gates set the pace

5 min live

The order is not a preference. A table can only be proved in the new warehouse once everything it is built from is there and proved. So retirements leave first, then the tables move in waves taken from the lineage: raw and reference tables, then staging, then marts, then the marts built from marts.

LiveWhere the waves come from3 min▶

Builder session 2 takes the 11 lineage edges from session 1's query log, removes the three retire candidates, and gives every table its wave: the longest path from a table nothing in the warehouse builds. On Serai's slice that is wave 0, the three marketplace exports, raw.reviews and ref.state_region; wave 1, stg.orders and stg.reviews; wave 2, mart.orders_daily, mart.brand_review and mart.seller_scorecard_v2; wave 3, mart.orders_monthly, which is built from orders_daily. The builders also check that all 11 edges point forward, from a lower wave to a higher one.

Two things to ask for as the leader. First, the output, not the method: the list of waves and the forward-edge check. Second, why brand_review sits in wave 2 although it reads a reference table directly. The answer is that it also reads both staging tables, so it waits until they are proved; a table moves after the slowest of its inputs, never the fastest.

LiveRetire first, and a gate after every wave2 min▶

Retirements go first because they shrink every later step. Left in the graph, mart.seller_scorecard_v1 would land in wave 2 as one more table to translate, reload and prove for a mart nobody reads. Retiring first means the first step of session 2's two-step retirement, stop and revoke with a date, is done on legacy before wave 0 begins.

A wave is finished when its tables are loaded in the new warehouse and pass the parity checks session 4 signs off, against the legacy copies. Only then does the next wave start reading them. If wave 1 fails, the cause is in its two tables, because wave 0 already passed. Note where Serai's two rebuilds sit: stg.orders in wave 1 and orders_daily in wave 2. Rebuilds take longer and carry more risk, and the gate stops a slow rebuild from being hurried by the wave behind it.

Self-studyWhen the wave plan meets the business calendar3 min▶

The graph gives the order, not the dates. Dates come from the business: nobody wants the wave that carries the monthly mart to finish on the morning of the finance close, or the board pack to change system in the week it is read. Lay the waves on the calendar and move a gate rather than reorder a wave: an order taken from the graph cannot be swapped without some table being proved before its inputs.

Isolated tables are the exception. A table with no edge in or out, read by people, can move in any wave, because nothing depends on it and it depends on nothing inside the warehouse. On Serai's slice both isolated tables are retire candidates, so the question does not arise; on a real estate it often does, and those tables are useful filler for a quiet wave.

Real world

An order written down before the change starts. Google's SRE book says "Almost all updates to Google's services proceed gradually, according to a defined process". That chapter is about releasing software, not moving data, so read it as an analogy, not as evidence about warehouses. The habit carries across: the order is written down before the first wave moves, so that nobody chooses the next step at eleven at night under pressure, and the scope sheet is that written order.

Build-along

Serai's scope sheet

★ 20 min · everyone builds

You need the inventory sheet from session 2 and the wave list from whoever runs the builder work, Builder session 2's output for Serai. No estate of your own to hand? Build Serai's with the rows below. The sheet becomes runbook section 2, signed.

Start from the signed inventory, not a fresh list. Session 2's sheet has 14 rows with a status, a reader and a signer. Carry the 3 retire candidates straight across as "retire", with the stop-and-revoke dates you already wrote. They take no further decision tonight.

Ask question 2 of every remaining row, and write the reason in one line. For each drift from session 1, ask the builders which table carries it and whether a rule fixes it or the design has to change. A rule means lift; a design change means rebuild.

Homework

Before session 4

25 min
  1. Take your inventory from session 2 and mark every non-retired table lift or rebuild. Count the rebuilds, and for each write the one-line reason. Your numbers will differ.
  2. For one rebuild, ask its owner whether any number it produces is meant to change. If yes, write that change on the done sheet as "explained", with an owner, before any parity run.
  3. Ask whoever runs your last migration, or your next one, whether it was one switch or phased, who decided, and on what reasons. Write down whether those reasons were about your estate or about a schedule.
  4. Find one dashboard on your done sheet that reads tables from more than one wave. Write down the earliest point its readers could move.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why is Serai's stg.orders a rebuild rather than a lift?

A rebuild is for a fault whose fix changes the design. Export C's lower-case keys and the missing review scores are fixed by a rule, so those tables stay lifts. Being read, or being busy in the graph, decides whether and when a table moves, not how.

2 · A board member says "the evidence shows a single switch is too risky, so we must phase it". What is the most accurate reply?

The disagreement is real. The Google Cloud blog's iterative move is a vendor claim; TSB is one programme that went badly. Neither settles the choice, so the scope sheet records it as a judgement, with a name and reasons.

3 · During the rebuild of mart.seller_scorecard_v2, the analytics lead wants to change how an active seller is counted. What is the right call?

An unannounced change looks exactly like a defect, and a defect can then hide behind it. C goes too far: a number may change, but only as a written, owned decision made before anyone looks at a diff.
