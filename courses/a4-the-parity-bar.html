The parity bar

Every parity report answers a question someone chose: how close is close enough, for which number, at which grain. If nobody chose it in advance, the person reading the diff chooses it on the night, under pressure, looking at the answer. Tonight you write the bar for the Constructed Serai Commerce example and sign it before any comparison runs: one line per metric, its tolerance, the accepted difference it absorbs, the defect it must still catch, and the person who signs it. Then you watch what happens to the same suite when the bar is exact, when it is signed, and when it is too loose.

Part 1 · covers accepted differences and the signed bar

Accepted differences, signed before anyone looks

7 min live

Two of Serai's seven drifts are not mistakes. The new warehouse splits freight across order lines and rounds per line, and it sums money as floating point. Both are reasonable engineering choices, both change numbers, and both will light up a comparison that demands exact equality. A bar exists so those two are absorbed by agreement, while the five defects are still caught.

LiveThe two differences Serai accepts, with their size3 min▶

Freight split per line. The new warehouse divides each order's freight across its lines and rounds each line to the cent. On 90 orders the pieces no longer add back to the original, and Serai's total freight moves from BRL 195,579.02 to BRL 195,579.59. Measured on the bench, freight differs on 80 days and stays inside the 0.05% bar in every month.

Money summed as floating point. Floating-point arithmetic stores most cents as a near neighbour, so sums differ in the last bits: item value on 302 days and freight on 269. The largest monthly gap is about 2e-10 BRL, far below a cent. Exact equality flags it; any tolerance clears it.

Accepting a difference is a decision with a size and an owner, not a shrug. Serai's financial controller signs the freight line knowing the size: 57 centavos across 90 orders. If the size were unknown, the line would be "open", not "accepted".

LiveWhy the bar is signed before anyone looks2 min▶

A tolerance chosen after the diff is in tends to be the one that makes the diff pass. That is not dishonesty; it is how anyone behaves at eleven at night with a cutover the next morning. The fix is procedural: the signer from session 2 signs each line of the bar, with a date, before the first parity run, and any later change to the bar is a new signature with a reason. The bar is a policy, so it is labelled as one: Serai's tolerances are constructed for the case, and yours will differ.

Three shapes cover most metrics. Counts (orders, reviewed orders) are exact, because a count has no rounding to forgive. Money gets a relative tolerance, because a cent on a small month and a cent on a large month are different sizes of doubt. Ratios and averages (items per order, average review) get an absolute gap in the unit people read, because 0.01 of a review star is what a brand lead would notice.

Self-studyTesting a tolerance against the defect it must catch3 min▶

Every line of the bar can be tested before it is signed: plant the defect it must catch, alone, and see whether the line fires. Serai's bar passes that test for each defect it names. Integer division moves items per order from 1.1427 to 1 in every month, far outside 0.005. NULL loaded as 0 fails reviewed orders in 20 of 21 months and average review in 19. The late rows fail orders in all 21 months.

Now test it the other way: plant each accepted difference alone and check the line stays quiet. At the monthly grain both do. Only a bar that passes both tests is worth a signature, and Builder session 10 runs exactly that pair of tests for every line.

Real world

Tools report differences; people decide which ones block. AWS documents that its Database Migration Service validation "compares each row in the source with its corresponding row at the target, verifies the rows contain the same data, and reports any mismatches", and that "Data validation requires that the table has a primary key or unique index." Read that as a vendor claim about the vendor's tool. Notice what the tool cannot know: whether a mismatch of 57 centavos on freight is a rounding policy or a defect. A report full of mismatches with no signed bar beside it moves the decision to the worst possible moment.

Part 2 · covers the trade between exact daily and a monthly bar

Exact every day, or within the bar every month

7 min live

Every parity headline should be two numbers, not one: defects caught, and false alarms raised. A suite that reports only the first can look perfect while the team learns to ignore it. The bench plants each drift alone and counts both, and the two middle rungs show the trade a leader has to make out loud.

LiveRung 2: everything caught, and two alarms nobody should act on3 min▶

Comparing six metrics on each of 598 days, exactly, catches all five defects. It also fires on both accepted differences: freight on 80 days from the per-line split, and floating-point money on 302 days of item value and 269 of freight. Those are 2 false alarms out of 2 accepted differences planted.

False alarms are not free. A team that sees freight fail every night for a month, and is told every morning "that one is fine", stops reading the freight line. On the night a real freight defect lands, the alarm looks exactly like the one they were trained to ignore. That is the cost leaders tend to underweight when they ask for "exact, everywhere, to be safe".

LiveRung 3: quiet, and blind inside the month3 min▶

Swap exact days for Serai's signed bar at the monthly grain the board reads, and the false alarms go to 0. So does one catch: 4 of 5. The fixed -03:00 offset ignores Brazilian summer time, so 72 real orders placed between midnight and one in the morning during the two summers in the sample land on the day before. That changes 74 daily order numbers. None of the 72 crosses a month end, so 0 months move, and a monthly bar cannot see it.

Serai's ops dashboard reads items per order daily, and the done sheet from session 1 records that grain. A monthly bar alone is therefore not enough for Serai, and the fix is not to go back to exact days. It is rung 4: one targeted edge check on the 595 orders placed within an hour of local midnight, which finds 72 of them on another day. 5 of 5 caught, 0 false alarms. That is the suite Serai signs.

Self-studyHashing every row or comparing aggregates: a live disagreement3 min▶

Practitioners disagree on whether aggregates are "enough", and the evidence does not settle it. Google's open-source Data Validation Tool offers both: column validation "(count, sum, avg, min, max, stddev, group by)" and row validation with SHA256 hashes. AWS DMS goes row by row and needs a key. Hashing every row finds a changed value that aggregates average away; it also flags every accepted difference row by row, so the freight split would show as 90 changed orders and floating-point money could show on every row that holds an amount, depending on how the hash is computed.

The honest answer is that it depends on the cost of a missed row, which is a business call, not a technical one. A ledger that must reconcile to the cent may justify row hashing with a rounding rule applied before the hash. A board pack read monthly may be well served by aggregates at that grain plus targeted edge checks. Write down which you chose and why, next to the bar.

Real world

A vendor's definition of done, iteration by iteration. A 2019 Google Cloud blog on migrating data warehouses asks teams, "At the end of each iteration", to "validate that the use case was successfully migrated according to your definition of done". Read it as a vendor claim, and notice what it leaves to you: the definition. The vendor's tools will compare whatever you point them at, exactly or within a tolerance; which grain, which tolerance and which false alarms you will live with is the bar, and only your signer can set it.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever: a bar too loose, and a comparison that compares nothing

When the bar is too loose, or the comparison is not one

6 min live

There are two easy ways to get an all-green parity report on a broken warehouse. Set one generous tolerance for every metric, or point both sides of the comparison at the same system. Both happen in real projects, both look like success, and the bench measures exactly what each one hides.

Live3% on everything: how a reasonable number hides a defect3 min▶

"Within 3% of legacy on all key metrics" sounds prudent in a steering pack. On Serai's bench it lets a defect through. When a missing review score is loaded as 0, every one of the 68 unreviewed orders starts counting as reviewed, with a score of zero. The largest monthly gap in reviewed orders is 2.22%, in February 2017. Under a 3% bar no month fails, so the drift is never seen. The suite drops from 4 of 5 to 3 of 5 at rung 3, and from 5 of 5 to 4 of 5 even at rung 4, because the edge check was built for time zones, not reviews.

Over the whole sample the same drift moves reviewed orders from 8,546 to 8,614 and the average review score from 4.1390 to 4.1063. A brand lead would notice the second number on her own dashboard; the parity report would tell her everything is fine. That is why each metric gets its own tolerance, set against the defect it must catch, rather than one comfortable number for all of them.

LiveThe comparison that compares nothing2 min▶

Point both sides of the suite at the new warehouse, which is what happens when a connection string is copied from one config to the next, and every check passes at every rung: 0 of 5 defects caught. Nothing in the report looks wrong. The only defence is in the report itself: every parity report Serai accepts prints both connection targets, the rung and the bar at the top, and the signer reads that header before the numbers. Builder session 10 has a break button so you can watch this happen.

Self-studyQuestions to ask about any green parity report2 min▶
  • What did each side read? Two named targets, not "source" and "target".
  • Which rung? Counts and keys alone caught 1 and 2 of Serai's 5 defects.
  • Which bar, signed when, by whom? A bar dated after the first run is a finding in itself.
  • At what grain? If anyone reads the number daily, a monthly pass is not the whole answer.
  • How many false alarms are being waved through? Each one is a line the team has learned not to read.
Real world

Readiness reported, and what came after. TSB's own release on the independent review of its 2018 migration notes that go-live "followed a long period of testing, with reports to the Board signalling readiness", and that the review raised "questions around how testing was carried out". In December 2022 the Bank of England announced fines of "a total of £48,650,000" and found that TSB "failed to organise and control the IT migration programme adequately." The release does not say a parity bar was the problem, and nothing here claims it was. What it shows is the gap a leader is paid to close: a report that signals readiness is only as good as the questions behind it.

Build-along

The bar sheet you sign

★ 20 min · everyone builds

You need your done sheet from session 1 and the signers from session 2. No sheet to hand? Build Serai's with the numbers on this page. The result is runbook section 4 from the leader's side: the policy every parity report is judged against, signed before the first diff.

Copy every number from the done sheet, with the grain it is read at. One line per number someone reads. Serai's are orders, item value, freight, items per order, reviewed orders and average review score. Items per order is read daily on the ops dashboard; everything else is read monthly.

Pick the shape of each tolerance before its size. Counts exact. Money relative. Ratios and averages an absolute gap in the unit people read. Then set the size, and label it as your policy: Serai's sizes are constructed.

★ Serai's tolerances (constructed policy, monthly)Orders exact Item value within 0.05% Freight within 0.05% Items per order within 0.005 Reviewed orders exact Average review within 0.01

Name what each line absorbs, with its size. Only differences someone has accepted in writing. Serai's freight line absorbs the per-line split, 90 orders and BRL 195,579.02 against BRL 195,579.59 in total; its money lines absorb floating point, largest monthly gap about 2e-10 BRL. "Nothing" is a valid and common answer.

Name the defect each line must still catch, and check the size against it. A tolerance wider than the drift it guards hides that drift: the missing-review drift's largest monthly gap is 2.22%, so a 3% bar would wave it through. For any number read daily, add a daily line for the risk you know about. Serai's is the edge check on the 595 orders placed within an hour of local midnight, signed by the head of operations.

Sign it, date it, and write the header every report must carry. The signer for each line is the signer from session 2. Then read the steering sentence to whoever approves the move.

★ The steering sentenceThe parity bar was signed line by line before the first comparison ran. Every parity report we accept prints both connection targets, the rung and the bar at the top, and reports defects caught and false alarms together. A change to the bar is a new signature with a reason.
A bar that reads well and hides a defect Parity criterion: all key metrics in the new warehouse within 3% of legacy. Exceptions to be reviewed case by case during cutover.
Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Write the bar sheet for your own five most-read numbers: grain, tolerance shape and size, what each absorbs, what each must still catch, and the signer. Your numbers will differ from Serai's.
  2. For one money line, find the accepted difference it must absorb (a rounding rule, a currency conversion, a floating-point sum) and estimate its size before you choose the tolerance.
  3. Take the last parity or reconciliation report your team produced and check its header: does it name both systems it compared, the grain and the tolerance? Write what is missing.
  4. Find one number on your done sheet that is read daily but checked only monthly or in total. Write the targeted daily check that would cover it, in the shape of Serai's edge check: which rows, compared how, alarm when.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Exact daily comparison catches all 5 of Serai's defects. Why does Serai not sign that as its bar?

Rung 2 is 5 of 5 caught with 2 false alarms. The fixed offset is caught at rung 2; it is the monthly bar at rung 3 that misses it. The cost of exact daily is the alarms it raises on differences Serai has already accepted.

2 · At rung 3, with the signed monthly bar, the fixed-offset drift is missed. Why?

Orders are checked exactly, so B is wrong, and the drift leaves rows and keys equal. No month moves, so a monthly bar sees nothing; the edge check at rung 4 finds 72 of the 595 near-midnight orders on another day.

3 · A steering pack proposes "within 3% on every metric". On Serai's rows, what does that do at rung 4?

The loose bar never improves the score. The signed bar already has 0 false alarms at rung 4, and 0 of 5 is the break, not the loose bar. The defect that slips through is the one whose gap is smaller than the comfortable number.
