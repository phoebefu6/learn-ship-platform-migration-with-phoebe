Ship It: Data Platform Migration · Leader session 2 of 6

Inventory before plan

Every migration plan starts with a list, and most lists start in the wrong place: with what exists. The useful list starts with what is read, by whom, and who will sign that it still says the same thing after the move. Serai Commerce owns 214 tables, and a quarter of a year of query log says 83 of them were not read once. Tonight you build the inventory sheet a leader signs before anyone draws a timeline: one status, one reader, one signer and, for the tables nobody reads, one retire date.

Part 1 · covers what you own and what is read

Count the reads, not the tables

7 min live

The size of a migration is not the number of tables in the old warehouse. It is the number of things someone would notice if they broke, plus everything those things are built from. Only a log of reads can tell you that number.

LiveSerai's estate, counted three ways4 min▶
What you countSerai (constructed)What it tells the plan
Tables that exist214 tables, 61 scheduled jobs, 38 dashboardsThe ceiling. Planning from it moves every mistake the old warehouse ever made.
Tables read in the last 90 days131 read at least once; 83 never readThe first honest cut. The 83 are retire candidates, not yet retirements.
Dashboards viewed in the last 90 days19 of 38 with no viewerA dead dashboard keeps its whole lineage looking "used", because its refresh job reads every table under it.

These are teaching numbers for the case. Nobody has published a reliable share of unread tables in a typical warehouse, and estates vary enormously, so treat any figure you hear quoted for "most warehouses" with suspicion. Your numbers will differ; the method will not.

LiveA read by a job is not a reader3 min▶

The 131 "read" tables include every table a scheduled job touched while building another one. A job reads a staging table every night whether or not anyone looks at what it builds. So a read count says a table is alive, not that it is needed. The question that sizes the move is narrower: is it read by a person or a dashboard, or does it feed something that is?

Builder session 1 answers that with one query that separates actors whose name starts with job: from everyone else. On its 14-table slice, 5 tables are read by people, 6 are read only by jobs and all 6 sit upstream of something people read, and 3 are not read at all. As a leader you do not run that query. You ask for its output, and you ask which window it used.

Real world

Vendors put the count before the plan. A 2019 Google Cloud blog on migrating data warehouses to BigQuery lays out its phases as "Prepare and discover", "Assess and plan" and "Execute". Read it as a vendor describing the process around its own product, never as a recommendation of that product. The order is still the right one: you cannot assess, still less plan, a move of things you have not counted, and the query log is the cheapest honest count you have.

Part 2 · covers who reads each number and who signs it

A reader and a signer for every number

6 min live

The done sheet from session 1 lists five numbers. Each one comes from a table, is read by someone, and needs one named person who will say "same" or "explained" on cutover day. A number without a signer is an open item before anyone has looked at a diff.

LiveReader and signer are different jobs3 min▶

The reader is whoever notices first when the number changes: the operations manager who opens the daily dashboard at nine, the analyst who pulls the scorecard. The signer is the person with the authority to accept a difference in writing: the one who can say "57 centavos of freight across 90 orders is a rounding policy we accept" and be held to it. Sometimes they are the same person. Often the reader is a team and the signer is its head.

Two rules keep the sheet honest. One signer per number, never a committee, because a committee cannot be asked a question at eleven at night on cutover day. And the signer is named before any parity check runs, because a person chosen after the diff is in tends to be the person who will accept it.

LiveThe reader the log cannot see2 min▶

Serai's done sheet says the finance close reads item value monthly, and the constructed query log in Builder session 1 has no finance reader at all. That is not a mistake in either: on Serai's case the finance team pulls a monthly extract into a spreadsheet, and the log records the export job, not the people who read the file. Partners who receive a nightly file, a quarterly audit pack and any report built outside the warehouse are invisible the same way.

So the inventory is built from two directions and they must meet. From the log up: what is read. From the done sheet down: what the business says it reads. Every number on the done sheet must trace to a table in the inventory. Any number that cannot is the most dangerous row on the page, because nobody will check it.

Real world

Readiness that was reported, not signed. TSB's own release on the independent review of its 2018 migration notes that the go-live "followed a long period of testing, with reports to the Board signalling readiness." The Bank of England later found TSB "failed to organise and control the IT migration programme adequately." A report that says "ready" is not the same thing as a named person per number saying "same, or explained by this". The inventory is where those names are written down, before there is a report to sign.

Part 3 · covers retiring what nobody reads

Retire before you move

6 min live

Every table you move costs a translation, a historical reload, a parity proof and a place in the parallel run. Every table you retire before the move costs a conversation and a date. The cheapest table to migrate is the one you switch off first.

LiveTwo steps, each one reversible3 min▶

Step 1, stop and revoke, with a date. Stop the job that writes the table, revoke read access, leave the data where it is, and announce the date to everyone who ever read it. If someone shouts, you grant access back in minutes, and you have found a reader the log missed. Step 2, archive and drop. After an agreed quiet period with no complaint, copy the table to an archive outside the warehouse and drop it. Undoing that is a restore from the archive: slower, still possible.

Do both steps before the move, on the legacy warehouse. A table retired after cutover has already been translated, reloaded and proved for nothing. The length of the quiet period is a judgement nobody has published a number for; Serai uses a constructed 60 days, long enough to cover one monthly close and its reruns, and you should set yours from the slowest reporting cycle you know of.

Self-studyWhy "no reads in 90 days" is where the conversation starts3 min▶

Ninety days is a window someone chose. It catches the daily and weekly readers and the monthly close; it misses anything read once a quarter, once a year, or once per audit. Serai's three retire candidates in Builder session 1 show the range: mart.seller_scorecard_v1 is written every night and read by nothing, replaced by scorecard_v2, so the table and its job retire together; mart.region_sales_2019 was last read by an old export job, so the question is where that job sends its file; tmp.orders_backup_2017 was read by an analyst in May, so you ask before you archive.

A wider window keeps more tables alive and makes the move bigger. A narrower one retires more and risks a quarterly report breaking. Neither is right by default. Pick the window from the slowest reader on your done sheet, write the choice into the inventory, and let each retire candidate's owner overrule it with a reason.

Self-studyScope belongs to the next session2 min▶

Tonight's statuses say what is read. They do not yet say how each table moves. Session 3 takes every "move" and "move as a dependency" row and decides whether it is lifted as it is, rebuilt for the target, or retired after all, and in which wave. Keep the two decisions apart: a table can be read every day and still deserve a rebuild rather than a straight copy.

Real world

A checklist beats a memory. Google's SRE book describes the launch checklist its launch coordination engineers use and reports that "The checklist proved to be a useful tool for ensuring reproducibly reliable launches." Retirement is a launch in reverse, and the two steps above are its checklist: who was told, which date, who can grant access back, where the archive lives. Write it once in the runbook and run it the same way for every table, so the third retirement is as careful as the first.

Build-along

The leader's inventory sheet

★ 20 min · everyone builds

You need the status list from Builder session 1, or from whoever owns your warehouse's query log, and your done sheet from session 1. No list to hand? Build Serai's: 14 tables, of which 5 move, 6 move as a dependency and 3 are retire candidates. The sheet becomes runbook section 1, signed.

Start from the status list, not the table list. Ask the warehouse owner for one row per table with its status and the window used. For Serai, Builder session 1's five queries produce it over a constructed 90-day log ending 2026-11-15.

★ Serai's statuses, from Builder session 1move (5) mart.orders_monthly, mart.orders_daily, mart.brand_review, mart.seller_scorecard_v2, stg.orders move as dependency (6) stg.reviews, raw.orders_export_a, raw.orders_export_b, raw.orders_export_c, raw.reviews, ref.state_region retire candidate (3) mart.seller_scorecard_v1, mart.region_sales_2019, tmp.orders_backup_2017 window 90 days, ending 2026-11-15

Give every "move" row a reader. Name the dashboard, report or person, as the log shows it. Then check the done sheet from session 1 against this column: every number on it must land on a table here. Serai's finance close does not appear in the log, so add it by hand to mart.orders_monthly and note "reads an extract".

Give every "move as a dependency" row the table it feeds. Its reader is whatever is downstream. If a job-only table feeds nothing anyone reads, it is not a dependency: move it to the retire column and say why.

Name one signer per row. For numbers people read, the head of the team that reads them. For dependencies, whoever owns the platform, because their sign-off is that the tables feeding someone else's number are complete. Write a role and a name; a team name is not a signer.

★ Serai's signers (roles constructed)mart.orders_monthly board pack, finance close (extract) finance director mart.orders_daily ops dashboard head of operations mart.brand_review brand review dashboard brand lead mart.seller_scorecard_v2 analyst_1 analytics lead stg.orders analyst_2 analytics lead 6 dependency tables feed the five above data platform lead

Date every retirement, in two steps. Stop-and-revoke date, quiet period, archive-and-drop date, and who can grant access back. A retire candidate whose owner is unknown gets "when owner found" and stays an open item.

★ Serai's retirements (dates constructed)mart.seller_scorecard_v1 stop and revoke 2026-12-01, retire job with it archive and drop after 60 quiet days mart.region_sales_2019 owner of job:old_export to be found first no date until then: open item tmp.orders_backup_2017 ask analyst_1; stop and revoke 2026-12-01 archive and drop after 60 quiet days

★ The steering sentenceWe plan the move from what is read, not from what exists. Every table on the inventory has a status, a reader and a named signer, and every number on the done sheet traces to one of them. Retire candidates are stopped and revoked on a dated plan before the move begins, and nothing is dropped without an archive.

Homework

Before session 3

25 min
  1. Ask your warehouse owner for three numbers: tables that exist, tables read at least once in the last 90 days, and dashboards with no viewer in the same window. Write down the window they used.
  2. Take every number on your done sheet and trace it to one table. Mark any number you cannot trace: that is a reader the log does not see.
  3. Name one signer for each number on the done sheet, as a role and a person. If two people claim a number, decide which one signs and write why.
  4. Pick one retire candidate and write its two-step retirement: who is told, the stop-and-revoke date, the quiet period and where the archive goes.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Serai's warehouse holds 214 tables. Why does the plan start from the 131 read in 90 days, and then from who read them, rather than from the 214?

A table that exists but is never read costs a translation, a reload and a parity proof for no reader. The 83 are retire candidates to confirm with owners, not tables to drop tonight, which is why A is wrong.

2 · No person or dashboard read ref.state_region in 90 days; only a job did. What does the inventory say?

A table earns its move through what is downstream of it. Retire it and the brand review dashboard's numbers break on cutover day, which is the failure the inventory exists to prevent.

3 · Why is "no read in the last 90 days" a judgement rather than a fact about a table?

The window is a choice that trades the size of the move against the risk of breaking a slow reader. There is no published standard window, so pick it from the slowest reader you know and let each owner overrule it with a reason.
